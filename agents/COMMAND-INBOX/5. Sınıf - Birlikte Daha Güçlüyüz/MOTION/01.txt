The camera holds almost still and drifts a few centimetres to the left, square to the window wall, just enough for the rumpled duvet in the near foreground to slide across the lower edge and open the opposite balcony a little wider behind her; the drift eases to a complete stop halfway through and does not move again. At first Mira sits exactly as she is, folded over her raised foot with both hands on the sock, her head already half turned to the glass and her eyes unfocused with sleep. Then, on the opposite balcony, the white sheet the neighbour is holding catches the draught and swings out wide toward the street. Half a second later Mira's eyes find it and settle on it — her lids lift, her brows draw a fraction closer and her mouth closes on the interrupted yawn. By the end her gaze is fixed out through the glass and awake, and nothing else about her has altered: she is still folded over the same foot, both hands still on the same sock, the same shoulder still forward. Her hands do not move on the sock, the sock does not travel over the heel, her foot does not reach the floor and her back does not straighten. The neighbour keeps her own grip on the sheet with the same two fingers and the peg stays in her mouth. The bedside lamp's warm pool stays exactly where it lies on the duvet, dust turns slowly through the window's cold band, and the curtain breathes inward once. Exactly two people from first frame to last, the girl and the neighbour, and nobody enters or leaves. The buzzer panel below stays a rigid plate at the same angle and its name strip keeps the characters it already has; the lettering does not morph, re-spell, wobble or gain any new word. Silent clip, no audio, no dialogue, mouth closed, no lip movement. No whip-pan, no shake, no snap-zoom, no camera warp.
