From just above the water film the camera rises with the haul — a short, fast lift of about a foot that accelerates on her pull and stops dead the instant the rug drops, then tilts down the rug toward the far end still lying in the water. At first she has the near end lifted a hand's width, water sheeting out of the fold, her arms locked and her eyes fixed on the far end. Then she hauls once, hard and fast — the near end comes up another two hands' width in one accelerating pull, the falling water thickening into a heavy rope. Then the weight beats her: her forearms give, the end drops back almost to where it started and slaps the water flat, her shoulders coming down with it. By the end her jaw is set and the rug lies lower than it did — she ends having tried and failed where she began still trying. The falling water swells from a stream to a rope and back to a trickle, the standing water is punched into rings that widen and flatten. Exactly one person is in the frame from first to last and no second pair of hands enters from any edge. The far end of the rug stays exactly where it lies, with the same outline and the same submerged fold. Her two hands keep the same fingers on the same rug edge throughout; the grip does not change. The bucket, the broom and the sacks stay rigid solids. Silent clip, no audio, no dialogue, mouth closed, no lip movement. No whip-pan, no shake, no snap-zoom, no camera warp.
